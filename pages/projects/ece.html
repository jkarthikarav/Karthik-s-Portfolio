At ECE at U of T UX/UI Design · Front-End Development Timeline: Nov 2024 – Mar 2025

Rebuilding the website to improve discovery, engagement, and partnerships for the University of Toronto's Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering

ECE's website didn't give prospective students a clear reason to explore its programs, and it wasn't working for faculty outreach or marketing. The goal wasn't to reinvent the site. It was to find where content discovery, student engagement, and partnership conversations could improve, while staying faithful to the department's brand. I conceived, designed, and rebuilt core parts of their public-facing website — reimagining the digital student experience, delivered as a responsive website across phone, tablet, and desktop.

Project Context

ECE's website was in need of user experience-related enhancements. The website wasn't giving students a clear reason to explore the programs, nor providing an attractive proposition.

Faculty members needed to use it for professional outreach. Marketing couldn't use it effectively, mainly because some pages such as Events were underdeveloped. The site was maintained by multiple teams, while branding alignment was not strictly enforced.

I researched, designed, and built the core pages myself, from Figma prototypes to live WordPress pages, while the admin, marketing, and programs teams owned content and approvals. Figma got each design through initial approval, and I then built the working drafts directly in WordPress, so stakeholders reviewed the real page with nothing lost in translation.

Reimagining the Student Experience

01     Made prospective students the primary audience.

Before changing any page, I needed to know who the site should work for first. I interviewed stakeholders, reviewed competitor department sites across North America, and talked informally with students and faculty.

Every audience mattered, but a page that works for someone with no context works better for everyone else too. That meant designing for two people. The first is the 17-year-old choosing a major, who wants to know whether the place is serious enough to matter and human enough to enjoy. The second is the graduate applicant, who needs institutional weight and research funding that justify years of commitment.

It also gave the team a shared test for the whole project: does this serve prospective students?

02     Rebuilding the homepage as independent sections, each pointing inward.

I restructured the Homepage from the ground up into independent modular sections, each optimized for a first-time visitor. The goal was a page that pointed clearly inward, to internal pages that were actually of value to the visitors.

My design focused on making it be perceived as open, active, and easy to engage with, instead of users having to hunt through disconnected sections.

03     Gave auto-generated and missing content a designated landing page.

Content that students should be able to browse was either displayed as default plugin or post output, or had nowhere to be accessed through (other than from a direct link).

For eg, Events was a standalone calendar and list plugin. I turned it into a structured page with category cards in a slider, contextual information, and the full calendar in an embedded widget. Visitors see the range of events first, then go into detail if they want it.

ECE Latest fixed news articles that existed only as individual posts, with no page to browse them. It is now a news and blog hub that shows the department is active.

04     Each page answers its main audience's question and also works as proof of opportunity for students.

Two pages were built for a specific audience, with prospective students in mind as secondary readers.

Industry Opportunities is an original page for industry partners, with "Work with us" and "Sponsor us" calls to action and departmental metrics. The department was active in industry but the site didn't show it. A prospective student reading the page sees which organizations the department connects them to.

Student Research Opportunities sits under the Research page, which had no way to host postings. Research access is a deciding factor for graduate applicants, so the page tells them the opportunities exist.

05     Built every page from components others could duplicate

Several teams maintained the site, and there was no shared component library, so pages kept rebuilding the same patterns from scratch. I built the pages above from components that non-designers can duplicate with copy edits, and made each one responsive so it holds up across phone, tablet, laptop, and desktop.

The clearest test came with TNFC, where I consolidated a standalone research-lab site into a two-page hub inside ECE's website. Those components were then reused for the Grid Modernization Centre's page after I left.

06     Responsive by design.

Whether accessed from a phone between classes or a desktop for research, the experience remains consistent across devices. Responsive layouts, flexible components, and adaptive content ensure the same intuitive navigation and visual hierarchy across phone, tablet, laptop, and desktop.

This approach transforms a website into an ongoing information guide — faster, clearer, and more valuable for both people and the educational institution — while respecting the trust users place in their university during some of the most important moments of their lives.

Reflections

Sites that look right at launch but are too fragile for non-technical staff will eventually break down. Thereore, reusable section patterns and components weren't a nice-to-have, it was to ensure the work would last beyond my involvement.

Designing with prospective students as the primary audience solved two problems at once. It gave us a design direction and a political one. Every content request could be evaluated against a single question: does this serve prospective students? When the answer was no, that was a reason, not a preference. It turned subjective pushback into a conversation with stakes.
