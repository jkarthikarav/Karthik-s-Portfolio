Zero Domain Knowledge to Successful Field Research in Two Weeks: UX Research Internship at Caterpillar

Caterpillar needed research on construction site supervisors. I had no construction industry knowledge, asked to be covert, and two weeks to produce findings worth presenting to a UX lead. My contract was extended by a month. This is what that process looked like.

Role: User Research Design & Synthesis Timeline: Dec 2019 Client: Caterpillar
The Brief

Learn an industry you know nothing about. Then research it without revealing who you work for.

The brief had two layers. The first was domain: I had no background in construction, no familiarity with how sites operated, and no prior knowledge of the workflows, hierarchies, or pressures that defined a site supervisor's day.

The second was approach: I was to conduct research with construction site supervisors without disclosing that I was a Caterpillar intern. The concern was straightforward; knowing who I worked for would change what people said.

Both constraints forced a kind of research discipline that a more comfortable brief wouldn't have required.

The Prep Work

I had to understand the industry before I could understand the people in it.

Before any field research could happen, I needed enough domain knowledge to ask credible questions and recognize meaningful answers.

I studied construction project structures such as initiation, planning, execution, closing; resource categories including workforce, equipment, raw materials; and scheduling, and the key metrics site supervisors were accountable for: duration, budget, safety, quality.

The Research

Covert research requires a different kind of trust-building.

When participants don't know who you work for, you can't rely on institutional credibility to open conversations. Trust has to be built through the quality of the questions and the genuineness of the listening. I approached site supervisors as someone trying to understand their work (which I was) and let the specificity of my domain preparation signal that I was worth talking to.

The research combined interviews with observation, empathy mapping across two distinct supervisor profiles, and user flow documentation that mapped how a supervisor's day actually moved versus how a planning or reporting tool might assume it moved. The gap between those two things was where the most useful findings lived.

The empathy maps in the documentation capture two supervisor types: one managing communication and task delegation across a site, one managing client relationships and progress reporting under pressure. Both pointed toward the same underlying need: a way to plan, track, and communicate progress that reduced the overhead without requiring excessive behavioral change from the people on site.

The Findings

Site supervisors don't need more data. They need the right data, at the right moment, without leaving the site to get it.

The specific findings I can speak to directionally: scheduling and task tracking were the highest-priority workflows. Resource tracking — equipment, labour, raw materials — was being done manually or not at all in real time. Client communication was reactive rather than planned. And the supervisors most under pressure were the ones managing the most variables simultaneously with the least system support.

The weeks following the research completion, I designed a mobile app and a SaaS Dashboard in Figma. Now, with the help of AI, I privately built them out in front-end code to demonstrate how they'd actually look and behave, not just how they'd appear in a static mockup. The SaaS Dashboard screens are currently active.

What Happened After

After presenting to the UX lead, my contract was extended by a month to shadow agile sessions. Now, I was watching how research findings move through a product team in practice.

The most important thing I learned on this project wasn't about construction. It was about domain fluency as a research skill. You don't need to be an expert in a field to do good research in it. But you do need to know enough to ask questions that earn respect and recognize answers that matter. Getting to that threshold and completing the UX research in two weeks was quite the challenge.
