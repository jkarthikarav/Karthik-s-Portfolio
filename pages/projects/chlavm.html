Realigning the Website Around Its First-Time Visitor: Pro Bono Sprints for CHLAVM

For a physical museum, the website is a supporting channel. For a virtual-only cultural institution, it is the entire experience. I led a discovery study to find out why it wasn't working for the people it was built for.

Deliverables: UX Research & Synthesis Timeline: Jan – Feb 2025 Client: Canadian Hispanic-Latin American Virtual Museums (CHLAVM)
The Problem

The website was the whole institution.

CHLAVM is a virtual-only museum (with VR exhibits) dedicated to preserving and sharing the heritage of Hispanic and Latin American communities in Canada. The website is the museum, which means every usability gap was a barrier to the institution itself.

The question we set out to answer was specific: is the CHLAVM website organized to support a first-time culturally curious user?

Note: this was a pro bono academic engagement. Nonprofits typically operate within funding cycles and grant processes. The decision to act on recommendations was theirs, our job was to make the insights rigorous enough that the findings would still hold when they chose to execute them.

The Current CHLAVM homepage.

The Research Approach

Instead of asking users what went wrong, We mapped what they expected.

Mapping mental models captures the gap between what users expect an interface to do and what it actually does. That is where the design opportunity lives.

Survey questions were built to understand navigation expectations rather than satisfaction ratings. Interview tasks were structured around specific actions such as finding an exhibit, locating resources, and donation. Why? So that we could observe where mental models diverged rather than asking participants to describe it.

Survey

18 responses mapping navigation expectations, content comprehension, and engagement patterns.

One-on-one remote interviews

Targeted usability tasks over Zoom, observing where users stalled, misnavigated, or disengaged.

Competitor analysis

Across three categories: virtual-only museums, physical museums with virtual exhibits, and Latin American cultural institutions.

Accessibility audit

Full WCAG compliance review of the current site identifying specific gaps across contrast, labeling, structure, and keyboard navigation.

Heuristic analysis

Landing page layout, content hierarchy, and navigation evaluated against established usability principles.

The Findings

These were systemic gaps, not isolated incidents.

Each finding pointed to a structural decision that was creating issues at every level of the experience.

Finding 1 — Navigation reflects internal logic.

The site's navigation was organized the way the museum thought about itself — not the way a first-time visitor would look for something. Users couldn't predict what they'd find before clicking, which meant every navigation decision was a guess.

Finding 2 — The landing page has no hierarchy.

Undifferentiated video lists and dense text-heavy copy produced cognitive overload before users had oriented themselves. There was no clear entry point, no signal about where to start, and no differentiation between content types.

Finding 3 — Critical actions had unnecessary steps.

Donation required a signup. Users found this confusing and in several cases disengaged from the action entirely. For a nonprofit that depends on donor relationships, this is a direct impact on revenue and trust.

Finding 4 — Content is presented without context.

Video exhibits had no titles, descriptions, or framing. Users couldn't evaluate whether something was worth their time before committing to it — which meant many didn't.

Finding 5 — The content is genuinely distinctive. The delivery system hasn't caught up to it yet.

CHLAVM has more culturally specific content than most museums in its category. The competitive analysis made this clear. The collection is strong — the infrastructure for delivering it has room to grow.

From Findings to Design

The research translated directly into component and page-level decisions.

The five findings didn't stay as a report. I designed components and page layouts in Figma that showed specifically how each gap could be addressed. Then I privately built them out in front-end code to demonstrate how they'd actually look and behave, not just how they'd appear in a static mockup.

Two pages are live as front-end implementations: the Education and Learning page and the Embassies and Consulates page. Each demonstrates a different treatment based on what the research said those pages needed to do.

What I Learned

The most important research decision on this project was the one made before any data was collected: designing the disposable UX artifacts that mapped expectations rather than collected complaints. Disposable? Yes, No one at CHLAVM was going to/needed to see the survey or analysis checklist after we were done with it. They needed the cold, hard facts.

The constraint of a pro bono engagement with a nonprofit client also focused the work. When you know recommendations may sit in a queue for months before action is possible, you write findings that are specific enough to still be actionable when the moment arrives. We all know that Vague insights age poorly, but really Precise ones hold.
