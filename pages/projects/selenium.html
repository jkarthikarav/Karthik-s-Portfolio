A Concept Direction for Non-Commercial Product Sites: Selenium

This is a self-initiated concept out of pure curiosity: a concept direction for a website that did not focus on making money through their open source products, needed to educate and support its users, and ALSO promote downloads for said open-source products.

01    Project Context
What does a homepage look like when it isn't selling anything?

That is what I was curious about when looking at the Selenium website.

Selenium doesn't need a checkout page. The journey is specific: get them to install one or all three of their OSS, learn how to use the tool, and support them if they're stuck. That's a different design problem than most SaaS sites solve — and it's the one I wanted to explore.

Existing Selenium website homepage.

02    Approach
For most Product and B2B/B2C homepages, the design language — hero sections, pricing tiers, start free trial CTA — is built for products trying to convert a visitor into a paying customer.

Before any field research could happen, I needed enough domain knowledge to ask credible questions and recognize meaningful answers. I studied construction project structures such as initiation, planning, execution, closing; resource categories including workforce, equipment, raw materials; and scheduling, and the key metrics site supervisors were accountable for: duration, budget, safety, quality.

Selenium isn't doing that. It's doing three other things at once:

Education — understand what browser automation even is

Support — pointing towards docs, forums, and help

Driving adoption — actually download and use the tools

03    Core Structural Idea
Selenium's homepage is the front door to three products, each with their own history and specifications. So I designed it as a landing page whose entire job is routing, not converting.

Under this direction, the three product cards are the primary navigation, each one linking out to its own dedicated homepage for WebDriver, IDE, and Grid. The homepage itself never needs to explain those products in depth, because it's supposed to be a well-organized table of contents.

The homepage can spend its space building trust and credibility instead of cramming in feature detail.

04    Product Story Without Showing the Product
Selenium already has the credibility markers most positioning decks would beg for — they just weren't visible anywhere on the homepage:

Selenium already has more than 34.2k GitHub stars, 8.7k forks, and 20 official repositories (and rising). Information that is nowhere on the current homepage.

I added a single stat band directly in the hero, before the product cards — so the very first thing a visitor reads after the headline is "this is the industry standard," not "here are three tools." It's one section, entirely built from numbers Selenium's own GitHub makes public.

05    Branding and Hierarchies
Most SaaS and developer-focused product sites right now default to the same look — soft gradients, blurred mesh backgrounds, glassy cards. It's become the generic signal for "AI product," and it actively works against a project like Selenium, which is older, more established, and more concretely useful.

The creative direction I explored instead: one saturated, confident accent per product — used identically in role and weight — so the differentiation reads as intentional categorization rather than three products built by three different people at three different times. I also paired with a dark, near-black hero and a warm, deliberate green as the single dominant brand color.

The current site also uses two different shades of blue to have two CTAs - to explore their partners and to donate. Meanwhile their key partner logos are displayed in huge sizes with a blog section in need of formatting.

07    Conclusion & Next Steps
Same copy, structure, and products. What changed is the pacing, the hierarchy of what needs space on the page, and a color and type system built for what Selenium is: an established, widely-used piece of developer infrastructure that happens to be free.

This was a case study on Creative direction for a product with no transaction to design toward, with no assumed stakeholder priorities
