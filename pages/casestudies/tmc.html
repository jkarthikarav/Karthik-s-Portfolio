IA and Navigation Menu Redesign

For the Textile Museum of Canada

    Presented Nov 2023 • Role: UX Researcher (Team Project) • Client: Textile Museum of Canada • IA + Mockups

Summary: Case Study detailing the Redesign of the Textile Museum of Canada Website’s Information Architecture and Navigation, which was presented to CEO Kirsten Kamper, and Caitlin Donnelly of The Textile Museum.

The Textile Museum of Canada approached us to help redesign the information architecture of its website to better promote key offerings. The focus was on improving the website’s information architecture to make content more accessible, highlight the museum’s values, and serve its target audiences.

During an information session on October 24, 2023, museum staff highlighted key issues: difficulty finding tickets and group tours, challenges accessing archived events, and a desire to showcase post-pandemic hands-on programs. Success metrics for the redesign include reduced bounce rate and increased online ticketing and donations.

We approached the problem by asking: How can the museum’s website structure better support discovery, engagement, and action?

Understanding Users

We began with a card sorting exercise. Eight participants grouped forty pages, from general information to events and programs, into categories that made sense to them. We used Optimal Workshop to collect data and observe patterns in user expectations.

Results showed shared expectations and areas of confusion. Pages like Privacy Policy and Meet the Team consistently grouped under About Us. Donation-related pages were separated. Ambiguous pages such as TXTilecity and Cloth Clay caused hesitation, showing the need for clearer labeling.

We also conducted a content audit of thirty-two pages. Each was evaluated for audience relevance, user expectation, recency, and clarity of calls to action. We found outdated information, inconsistent labeling, and unclear guidance for volunteers, job seekers, and museum visitors. These insights highlighted where IA and navigation redesign would have the most impact.

Testing out the IA

To validate our proposed Information Architecture, we conducted a small-scale tree test using OptimalSort with 10 participants who completed 6 navigation tasks. Our goal was to understand how users interpreted and located content within the proposed structure, separate from the website’s existing menu design.

Results

30%

of users located the Collection Gallery under “Explore,” suggesting that section was too deep and complex.

80%

of users achieved indirect success when identifying “past” content, showing ambiguity between similar categories.

40%

of users selected Ways to Donate instead of Donate Your Textiles, indicating unclear naming conventions.

Strategy in Action

Phase 1: Test New IA in Existing Menu

Integrate the new, consolidated IA into the current menu structure with minimal visual changes. Gather user feedback and analytics to assess improvements in navigation and content discoverability.

Phase 2: Test New Navigation Menu Design

Move away from the hamburger menu to a visible, mega menu-styled navigation bar. Gather user feedback and analytics to assess improvements in navigation and content discoverability.

Ensuring Data-Driven Adjustments after Implementation

While the card sort and tree testing were done by the entire team, I personally created the visual navigation menus, informed by our research. While aiming to isolate cause and effect, I could get genuinely actionable next steps for stakeholders. By implementing IA separately from the navigation menu's redesign, we can attribute improvements in user success to the correct intervention. This approach, while more complex, prevents wasted resources, builds client trust, and ensures that future design decisions are always data-driven.

What I Learned

The goal of this project was to create a clear, accessible IA to help people discover and navigate the Textile Museum of Canada’s collections, exhibitions, and events.

The project strengthened my ability to prioritize features and content for maximum impact, showing that building trust online comes from a simple and transparent digital experience.

Key Takeaway: User Needs AND Business Goals, not OR

A phased plan ensured user needs and business goals were met, with measurable improvements clearly linked to specific changes.
