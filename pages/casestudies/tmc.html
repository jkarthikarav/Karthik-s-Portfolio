IA and Navigation Menu Redesign

For the Textile Museum of Canada

    Presented Nov 2023 • Role: UX Researcher (Team Project) • Client: Textile Museum of Canada • IA + Mockups

Summary: Case Study detailing the Redesign of the Textile Museum of Canada Website’s Information Architecture and Navigation, which was presented to CEO Kirsten Kamper, and Caitlin Donnelly of The Textile Museum.

The Textile Museum of Canada approached us to help redesign the organization of information on its website to better promote key offerings. The focus was on improving the website’s information architecture and navigation to make content more accessible, highlight the museum’s values, and serve its target audiences.

During an information session on October 24, 2023, museum staff highlighted key issues: difficulty finding tickets and group tours, challenges accessing archived events, and a desire to showcase post-pandemic hands-on programs. Success metrics for the redesign include reduced bounce rate and increased online ticketing and donations.

Research Process

Card Sorting

To understand how potential visitors would intuitively browse the Textile Museum website, we conducted an open card sorting study with 8 participants using Optimal Workshop. We selected 40 pages covering general information, events, activities, and museum programs to focus on content relevant to visitors interested in booking tickets or learning about the museum. Participants were asked to group the pages and assign category names, allowing us to see natural patterns in how users expected information to be organized.

The card sorting revealed both common trends and unique approaches to categorization. Most participants consistently grouped general information pages such as Privacy Policy and Meet the Team under categories like About Us. Donation-related pages were often grouped separately, while participant 4 used broader categories, highlighting differences in how users approach hierarchical organization. The study also surfaced ambiguous or unfamiliar pages, such as TXTilecity and Cloth Clay, where participants hesitated or expressed uncertainty.

While the sample size was small and limited to a single-level sort, the study provided useful insights for structuring the new Information Architecture. The findings highlighted patterns of shared understanding while pointing out areas of confusion, which informed how we proposed categories and labels for the redesigned navigation.

Content Audit

To evaluate the Textile Museum website, we analyzed 32 pages using four criteria: Audience, User Expectation, Recency & Relevancy, and Call to Actions. These criteria helped us assess the value of each page for visitors and identify gaps in organization, clarity, and actionable content. While most content was informative and up to date, we found issues with inconsistent titles, outdated indicators, and unclear calls to action.

Our audit revealed that several pages lacked clear information about volunteer and internship opportunities, including details about requirements, benefits, and whether positions were currently available. Additionally, some pages, such as those related to health and safety or museum reopening, did not indicate if the content was still relevant. Navigation and page labels were sometimes inconsistent, making it harder for users to locate important information quickly.

We summarized the core findings to inform improvements in labeling, organization, and calls to action for the redesigned website. Addressing these issues will help visitors find relevant information efficiently and take the desired actions, such as booking tickets, exploring membership options, or applying for volunteer roles.

Ensuring Data-Driven Adjustments after Implementation

While the card sort and tree testing were done by the entire team, I personally created the visual navigation menus, informed by our research. While aiming to isolate cause and effect, I could get genuinely actionable next steps for stakeholders. By implementing IA separately from the navigation menu's redesign, we can attribute improvements in user success to the correct intervention. This approach, while more complex, prevents wasted resources, builds client trust, and ensures that future design decisions are always data-driven.

I proposed a phased implementation and testing strategy:

Phase 1: Test New IA in Existing Menu

Integrate the new, consolidated IA into the current menu structure with minimal visual changes. Gather user feedback and analytics to assess improvements in navigation and content discoverability.

Phase 2: Test New Navigation Menu Design

Move away from the hamburger menu to a visible, mega menu-styled navigation bar. Gather user feedback and analytics to assess improvements in navigation and content discoverability.

The Strategy

Phase 1:

The short-term solution that will integrate the new consolidated information architecture into the existing navigation menu design. This will allow testing with real users and gathering metrics, while avoiding disruption from a complete overhaul of the navigation system. It will also address accessibility issues.​

Phase 2:

The long-term solution that brings the menu out of the hamburger. This approach also allowed for more flexible use of color, to improve the design using the WCAG contrast and accessibility guidelines.

Validation

To validate our proposed Information Architecture, we conducted a small-scale tree test using OptimalSort with 10 participants who completed 6 navigation tasks. Our goal was to understand how users interpreted and located content within the proposed structure, separate from the website’s existing menu design. These findings are not part of the phase 1 and phase 2 testing outlined in the strategy I developed, but come from our own evaluation of the IA with a smaller sample. While the results provided useful insights, I believe the current navigation menu’s visual design could still influence real-world outcomes.

What I Learned

The goal of this project was to create a clear, accessible IA that makes it easier for people to discover and navigate the Textile Museum of Canada’s rich collections, exhibitions, and events.

This project also strengthened my ability to prioritize features and content, focusing on what would have the most impact for both first-time visitors and returning patrons. I found that building trust online comes from making the digital experience simple and transparent.

Key Takeaway: Blending User Needs with Post-deployment Business Goals

The most valuable insight was learning to balance immediate user needs such as content discoverability with the museum’s long-term goals, such as increasing engagement and supporting their growth.

Key Takeaway: Phased, Research-driven Planning

Implementing a phased plan ensured that both user needs and organizational goals were addressed, allowing measurable improvements to be clearly attributed to the correct changes.
