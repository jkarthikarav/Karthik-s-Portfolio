Textile Museum of Canada Website

IA Redesign & Strategy

For: Textile Museum of Canada

Study Conducted Oct - Nov 2023

Summary: The museum needed to improve their site's content discoverability. While the conventional solution was a UI overhaul, I proposed a phased implementation strategy to isolate improvements, ensuring the right attribution.

The Textile Museum of Canada faced a disconnect between their rich content library and user engagement.

The initial request to our MI cohort was broad: "Fix the architecture."

The focus of this project is to redesign the website's information architecture and navigational design to promote the museum's values better and showcase information for their targeted audiences in an accessible way.

The museum needed to make ticket sales, donations, and archived events easier to find, while also highlighting the return of in-person and hands-on programs post-pandemic.

Research: Understanding the Mental Model

Before proposing solutions, we needed to validate the problem. We used open card sorting and tree testing to study the existing architecture. We selected entirely new users because testing with existing users could be misleading. If existing users had workarounds for problematic architecture, their patterns and behaviors would cloud the test results.

Abstract labels like "TXTilecity" and "Cloth Clay" had high failure rates. The issue wasn't the jargon itself, but a lack of context, i.e, descriptive context to persuade users to explore or discover what these sections contained.

Users consistently grouped "Privacy Policy" and "Team" under "About Us," validating standard web conventions.

Audit & Data Gathering

Our initial audit and card sorting highlighted that the museum's digital presence was hampered by organizational inconsistency and a lack of clear direction.

Key Data

• 4-10 categories created on average by participants, showing diversity in content organization.

• 3 areas with incomplete or unclear information: volunteering, job/internships, and health & safety.

• Key page (“Visit”) lacked a clear call to action to encourage visitors to book tickets or explore membership options.

Testing the Existing Information Architecture

To assess whether the issue was structural, we conducted extensive Tree Testing on the existing site’s IA, stripping away all visual design to ensure failures were purely semantic.

Our data confirmed that deep hierarchy, navigational ambiguity, and label conflicts were actively blocking the user journey:

Key Data

• 30% of test users located the Collection Gallery under Explore.

• 80% of test users achieved indirect success when identifying Past content.

• 40% of test users were confused by “Ways to Donate” & “Donate Your Textiles” - We renamed the latter to “Making a Textile Donation”

Strategy: The Recommendation

Based on the data, we developed a new Information Architecture. However, simply handing an IA map was not enough for us. My teammates and I had a disussion where we felt like we needed to ensure this was practical and not theoretical.

The Risk of a Overhaul

The conventional wisdom that most of our Cohort mates also suggested (other teams working on the same issue) was to launch the New IA inside a new "Mega Menu" UI simultaneously. I advised against this due to the risk of misunderstanding what actually works.

If metrics changed after a simultaneous launch, no one could attribute the change to the structure (IA) or the interface (UI).

What I proposed

I designed a phased A/B testing roadmap with interface mockups. This strategy allows them to validate our proposed IA before committing to development.

Phase A (Validation): Implement the New IA labels within the existing menu design to reduce UI bias.
Phase B (Optimization): Once the IA is validated, roll out the Mega Menu UI.

Our UI/UX Solution

The New Information Architecture:



Explore how our IA addressed stakeholder objectives

Plan Your Visit

We restructured the IA sub-tree for "Plan Your Visit" to provide direct paths to revenue actions: Admissions and Book a Group Tour. The existing site relied on a single "Visit" page with an in-page anchor menu. This forced users to scroll through eight sections: Admissions, Holiday Hours, Upcoming Programming, Members, Getting Here, Community Access, Accessibility, and Tour, to find the information they needed. The sub-level content shows the items users were most likely trying to access.

Pictured below: Plan Your Visit Section

The new structure elevates Admissions and Book a Group Tour to primary sub-levels and links directly to the corresponding conversion forms. This supports the stakeholders’ goal of making logistical information easily accessible, particularly for older audiences, by replacing long, anchor-link pages with a clear, shallow hierarchy that separates logistics from exploration.

Explore

We restructured the IA sub-tree for "Explore" to provide direct paths to "Current," "Upcoming," and "Past" exhibitions and events. The existing site relied on a single "What's On" menu item, forcing users to filter through paginated months and tags on one page to find specific content. The sub-level content shows the items users might be looking at.

Pictured below: Explore Section

The new structure allows each menu item to link directly to a pre-filtered results page, supporting the stakeholders' goal of faster event discovery and booking. By building the IA around the museum's existing tag and filter logic, the navigation can automatically update as new exhibits are added. This reduces technical maintenance and allows the team to focus on content creation rather than navigation upkeep.

This design satisfies the museum's requirement for better archival visibility by providing a dedicated home for Past Events, making them "easily accessible" without the need for manual filtering.

Post-Presentation Feedback

Our IA course instructor told us that our two-phased approach "stood out" among all groups -- context: this was a group project for all UX students in our year. Standing out was not easy, as we presented later in the schedule and we knew that the Textile Museum Board members had already seen multiple groups discuss their suggestions for revamping the IA.
Part of why we were noticed was because we provided stakeholders with a handout summarizing our research findings and navigation suggestions on the day of the presentation -- right before our slot, allowing them to review and take the insights home.
We were also told that we were "the first group" to study the current navigation menu in depth, identifying why it wasn't accessible, why it was not suited for content discovery, as well as identifying risks in a full UI overhaul and showing how it could be detrimental to content discovery.

“Loved giving us multiple options; to give us a short-term solution, really thoughtful for our current situation.”

-- Textile Museum Board, feedback delivered to our group through the Professor
