Discovery Study for Website Redesign

Diagnosing systemic website usability failures in Canada's only Hispanic-Latin American virtual museum (CHLAVM).

—

CHLAVM is a virtual-only museum with no physical presence; their website is their entire institution. We undertook a discovery study to produce design-directive intelligence to guide a full website redesign.

My role spans Research Design, Survey Instrumentation, Competitor & Accessibility Analysis, and Synthesis & Reporting

The CHLAVM landing page was structurally misaligned with how its users think, navigate, and make decisions online. This study, combines secondary analysis with targeted primary research across 22 participants, to identify systemic failures and produces the design-directive evidence needed to resolve them.

Disclaimer: This was a Pro bono capstone engagement, while I was a Master's student at the University of Toronto's Faculty of Information

Timeline: Jan 23 – Feb 12, 2025

Client: Canadian Hispanic-Latin American Virtual Museum

Methods: Survey, Interviews, Accessibility Audits

Team: 4 UX Researchers

Summary of Findings


—

The Canadian Hispanic-Latin American Virtual Museum is a virtual-only cultural institution dedicated to preserving, promoting, and sharing the heritage of Hispanic and Latin-American communities in Canada through interactive exhibits using virtual reality. This report tells you where their infrastructure breaks, why it breaks, and exactly how to fix it.

1

The navigation reflects the museum's internal logic, especially Labels like "Heritage Chronicles" and "Digital Playground", which meant users could not predict what they would find before clicking.

2

The landing page has no hierarchy, so everything competed equally for attention. This included an overcrowded hero banner, text-heavy sections, and undifferentiated video lists producing cognitive overload.

3

Critical actions are effectively invisible. For eg, Donation requires login , something users found confusing and off-putting for what they expected to be a simple act of support.

4

Content is presented without context. For eg, Video exhibits are listed without titles, descriptions, or framing.

5

CHLAVM has richer and more distinctive cultural content than museums such as The Royal Ontario Museum, but they simply lack the system to deliver it.

Why is a website redesign critical for CHLAVM?

From a systems perspective, for a physical museum, the website is a supporting channel. Often a way to plan a visit, browse a collection, buy a ticket.
For CHLAVM, the website is the entire experience. Every user who lands on the homepage and leaves without engaging with the VR exhibits represents a complete failure of the institution's mission.
This elevates the landing page from a design problem to a strategic question.

How useful should we consider this report, as your changes have not been implemented?

Having worked with various nonprofits, especially those in North America, I can reveal a few standard operating realities of the sector.
Nonprofit cultural institutions operate within funding cycles, board approvals, and grant processes that determine when and how recommendations can move to execution.
This was a pro bono capstone engagement through the University of Toronto's Faculty of Information, so while we conducted the research with full professional rigor, the decision to act on it is an organizational one. If I was paid to do this solo, I would have developed the website within 2 months of approval.

Research and Approach


—

Problem Statement

The core problem this study set out to investigate is both aesthetic and systemic. There is a gap between how CHLAVM understands and organizes itself internally and how a first-time culturally curious Canadian actually reads, interprets, and navigates the page.

Research Design

Most UX research asks users what is wrong. This study was designed differently. We already knew the landing page needed to change. Specifically: what should go where, and why.

The survey was designed to be design-directive rather than diagnostic. Instead of asking users to report their frustrations, we asked questions that mapped user mental models directly to interface decisions. Where do you expect to find social media links? Which navigation label would you click to get involved with the community? Does this video section tell you enough to decide whether to watch?

These questions generate evidence that closes the gap between research and design. Each answer corresponds to a specific interface decision: placement, labeling, hierarchy, context.

Competitor Analysis of digital platforms across three categories: virtual-only museums, physical museums with virtual components, and Hispanic-Latin American cultural heritage institutions.

Accessibility Audit of the CHLAVM website against WCAG standards identified compliance failures including non-responsive links, insufficient color contrast, etc.

Heuristic Analysis of the landing page's layout, content hierarchy, and navigation to establish a baseline of specific design failures.

Survey (18 responses) to generate design-directive responses about navigation, content, and engagement.

One-on-one remote Zoom sessions with targeted usability tasks including exhibit discovery, community engagement, and locating media.

Key Findings

The navigation reflects the museum, not the user

—

The CHLAVM navigation used internally-understood labels such as "Heritage Chronicles," "Digital Playground," and "Inspiring Legacies", but users could not predict what existed under these.

During interviews, participants struggled to locate exhibits, community content, and donation pathways. Survey responses showed similar struggles.

Core Research Finding: Users could not confidently predict where exhibits were located.

Design Implication: Navigation labels and hierarchy should reflect user tasks and mental models.

The home page has no hierarchy

—

The landing page presented multiple dense content blocks at equal importance. Users received no clear idea about where to begin or what mattered most.

Survey respondents described the page as overwhelming, outdated, and excessively long to scroll through. Interview participants frequently expressed uncertainty.

User Feedback: One Participant compared the page to "a bunch of slideshows put together."

Design Implication: Introduce progressive disclosure and hierarchy.

Critical actions are buried

—

Three critical actions: viewing exhibits, donating, and community engagement, were consistently difficult for users to locate.

Most Donation Management systems are simple to embed and straightforward to use. While Participants expected donation to be immediate, they had to login onto CHLAVM first.

Core Survey Finding: 72.2% of respondents expected social media links in the footer.

Design Implication: Donate, follow, and community actions should be primary calls-to-action.

Content is presented without context

—

For Video sections, Users encountered rows of thumbnails without titles, descriptions, or guidance explaining what they were about to engage with.

Participants consistently reported that they relied entirely on thumbnails and icons to make decisions, which was insufficient for culturally specific educational material.

Core Research Finding: Users could not determine video content from thumbnails alone.

Design Implication: Every content unit should include titles & summaries.

Users have a clear benchmark

—

When asked to compare CHLAVM with the Royal Ontario Museum website, users consistently preferred the ROM experience because of its cleaner layout, hierarchy, and intuitive navigation.

Users were not comparing CHLAVM to other virtual-only museums, they were comparing it to the standard museum website experience they already understood.

Participants still value CHLAVM's exhibits and cultural specificity. The issue was not the quality of the content itself, but the system.

OPPORTUNITY AREAS

—

The core problem this study set out to investigate is both aesthetic and systemic. There is a gap between how CHLAVM understands and organizes itself internally and how a first-time culturally curious Canadian actually reads, interprets, and navigates the page.

1

Realigning the navigation system to user mental models is the biggest opportunity because navigation failure affects every other user goal on the site.

2

Introduce progressive disclosure as the organizing principle reduces overload by ensuring the most important content and actions are seen first.

3

Make Donating, following, and community participation the path of least resistance by ensuring they get seen clearly, removing unnecessary barriers, and labeling them using industry-standard language.

What does this strategy look like when implemented?

The research was designed to be directive. The following shows how specific findings translated into design decisions and how those decisions work at both component and page level.

Content Cards

What it looks like in the Education and Leanring page

The redesign needed to solve not just labeling but the entire system of how pages announce and orient users on arrival.
A user arriving at Education & Learning needs to understand they are at a hub with multiple content types beneath it. But a user arriving at Embassies & Consulates (a subpage inside Community) needs to understand they are at a specific functional page with a specific task to complete.
The solution is a two-tier banner system that reflects the hierarchical relationship between pages.
While the Education and Learning page uses the Page Banner title component, the Embassies and Consulates subpage uses the Sub page banner.
