The Context

I led production design across the public website and intranet. From June 2024 to March 2025, I handled accessibility fixes and web production requests, tightening user journeys to align with both WCAG standards and U of T's Brand guidelines.

The Challenge

ECE's website served everyone. That meant it worked for no one in particular... least of all, the Students.

93% of high-school students use a university website during their decision-making journey. [Source]

ECE's wasn't built for them. The homepage had no clear primary audience. Prospective students with the highest stakes had no clear path through any of it.

Three pain points shaped the scope: outdated content, broken user journeys, and visual misalignment with the broader U of T brand.

Examine Audit/Discovery
Firstly, I examine.

Involves Content Audits and Discovery, wherein I understand common industry patterns/mental models and identify content gaps.

I didn't design the homepage in isolation. Redesigning it without addressing the pages it linked to would have sent prospective students to dead ends faster. An audit of ECE and comparable engineering department websites revealed multiple insights.

Visually, ECE's site was inconsistent with U of T at large. Content overlapped, but entire content categories were absent. For eg, UBC's Engineering asked for capstone proposals. Waterloo's was patent-focused. U of T's ECE did not have one.

We decided to prioritize first-time users and treat prospective students as the primary audience, with elements/content for other audiences needing to also serve them. Any content that couldn't was deprioritised.

My Reworked Content Architecture was going to link to Industry Opportunities, Student Research Opportunities, and ECE Latest (News). Only, None of those pages existed.

SYNTHESIZE DESIGN
Secondly, I design.

Involves synthesizing and designing the solution, ensuring its not just functional but also manageable.

With the infrastructure in place, I designed the homepage. Prototypes and user flows were mapped against the reworked Content Architecture.

Feedback came from two directions simultaneously: administration raised maintainability concerns, marketing wanted campaign flexibility in the hero.

Every piece of feedback was incorporated and the reasoning documented, and nothing in the final design was arbitrary.

Project Outcomes

—

My decisions and designs led to

Improved Discovery: Longer session times and stronger traffic flow to internal pages.

Reduced Time-to-Value: Faster access to academic and program information for high-intent visitors.

Marketing Impact: Hero section supports rotating campaigns without structural changes.

What I Learned

A great homepage pointing to underdeveloped internal pages would have replaced a solvable problem with a structural one.
Sites that look right at launch but are too fragile for non-technical staff to update will eventually break down.
Academic Institutions often design primarily for maintainability, resulting in sites not optimised for the people who need them.
Designing with prospective students as the primary audience solved both problems at once. Every request could be evaluated against whether it served prospective students. When the answer was no, designers can push back rather than quoting subjective preferences.
