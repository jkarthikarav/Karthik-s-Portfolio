The Challenge

ECE had a website that just wasn't working for the people who needed it most: the students.

93% of high-school students use a college’s or university’s website during their decisionmaking journey. [Source]

Many 2024 studies gave similar insights; for the ECE, this meant rebuilding the student-facing web presence would be a high-value project.

From June 2024, I was handling a steady flow of content updates, accessibility fixes, and production requests across the public website and intranet. That work ran continuously across the entire nine months through a weekly Asana workflow, alongside everything else.

The Karthik Approach

Before designing anything, I examine. This typically involves a Content Audit and Discovery Research, to define industry-standard patterns, establish a hierarchy, and identify content gaps.

The homepage was the most visible problem, but it wasn't the only one. The internal pages it was meant to link to were equally underdeveloped.

Redesigning the home page without fixing what was behind it would have sent visitors to dead ends faster.

With visual design directly influencing brand perception, the solution had to look like it belonged. I started with a content audit of the site and a review of UofT and non-UofT engineering websites; ECE was visually inconsistent with all of them.

The consistent finding: Content across institutions is largely similar, but presentation determines value. For eg, taking Industry-focused content: UBC treated capstone proposals as a submission, while UWaterloo's pages were text-heavy and patent-focused.

Most key design decisions were guided by stakeholder queries and requests, including internal teams such as department administration, co-op, undergraduate, graduate, and events to understand what each team needed the website to do for them.

Three decisions came out of that process: Prioritize first-time users over information density; Treat prospective students as the primary audience, with any content for other groups needing to serve students first; and Use progressive disclosure rather than trying to surface everything at once.

Industry Opportunities was a first-of-its-kind page for ECE.

I used a two-column card layout to immediately separate "Work with us" from "Sponsor us," so industry stakeholders did not have to read through everything. Departmental metrics were integrated as credibility signals, and the call to action was framed as a professional inquiry rather than a passive web form. A page built for industry partners still needed to make students feel like the department had their future in mind. Visibility into industry partnerships tells prospective students that the department is invested in their employability.

The Karthik Approach

Second, I design. This involved synthesizing insights and designing the solution, not just functional but also manageable.

With all three pages live, I moved to the homepage.

I built wireframes and user flows to map the new content architecture, then brought them to departmental leadership for review.

Feedback came from multiple directions: administration flagged maintainability concerns, marketing pushed for campaign flexibility in the hero, and students informally confirmed that research and co-op visibility mattered to how they perceived the department.

Every piece of feedback was incorporated and the reasoning documented, so nothing in the final design was arbitrary. The page was restructured into independent modular sections.

Multiple admins across different teams update the homepage regularly, supporting long-term scalability and ensures the site remains maintainable.

Previously, we had discussed about how the Events page was simply a default WordPress calendar widget sitting on a page with no context around it.

After the homepage went live, I flagged the Events page to administration, and they agreed it needed fixing, and I redesigned it.

Even though the mockup was approved, limitations with the widgets available led to a slightly different visual build.

The redesigned Events page gave upcoming events the context they were missing. It was scannable, student-friendly, and straightforward enough for staff to update without a developer.

What I Learned

Redesigning the homepage first would have been the obvious move. It was the most visible problem and the one everyone wanted fixed. But sending traffic from a better homepage to underdeveloped internal pages would have replaced one problem with another.
The second lesson was about designing for the people who maintain a system, not just the people who use it.
A homepage that looks right at launch but is too fragile for non-technical staff to update will regress within months.
Designing for a single primary audience also turned out to be the most practical tool for resolving stakeholder conflict. Every request could be evaluated against one question: does this serve a prospective student? When the answer was no, there was a principled reason to push back rather than a subjective preference.
