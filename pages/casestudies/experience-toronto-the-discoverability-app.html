Ticket Discoverability App

Structuring Information Flow for Cultural Access

Study Conducted Oct - Dec 2023

Summary: Mobile app to centralize ticket discovery and reduce affordability barriers for Toronto residents accessing GLAM institutions.

A city rich in culture, yet hidden behind paywalls and bureaucracy.

Our Hypothesis: Residents struggle due to systemic information fragmentation. We aimed to centralize discovery via a mobile app, simplifying access to GLAM discounts and events.

Before designing a solution, we studied the market. Many people discovered free museum days only by chance. Free and discounted admissions do exist, but they remain underutilized because information is scattered and poorly communicated.

We reasoned: If awareness is the problem, the solution must centralize discovery.

Asking the right questions

We surveyed 42 participants (36 valid responses) and conducted 12 in-depth interviews with Toronto residents interested in visiting museums. Our goal was to understand how people discover tickets and discounts, and what barriers prevent easy access. Our key questions:

1. How can museum admission information be made more visible?
2. How do people feel about ticket prices?
3. How aware are they of discount programs?
4. How do accessibility and affordability influence attendance?

What we found

We found that participants actively searched for discounted tickets but described the process as frustrating and time-consuming. The median effort rating was 2.14/5, and over half said easier access to deals would make them visit more often. Interviews confirmed that scattered information and ticket purchase friction discouraged attendance.

Turning research into design priorities

To convert insights into features, our team used an Impact vs. Feasibility matrix to prioritize ideas.

Key Design Directions

Ease of Access

Users want one-stop access to all available deals.

Plan & Share

Mary and her friends plan their visits in advance, so share function and calendar views matter.

Recommendations

Because Personalization encourages repeat app use.

Designing the experience

User Flows

The first step was to define the task flow, ensuring Mary could transition from finding a deal to planning her visit. We focused on ensuring users could successfully locate & save information without informational/cognitive overload.

Low Fidelity UI

Our low-fidelity prototype, designed in Figma, was presented to a panel of senior UX researchers through the University of Toronto’s iSchool during our course INF1602 Design Playback sessions.

Testing & Validation

We tested our low-fidelity prototype and our planned task flows using moderated remote sessions via Zoom with participants aged 18+ interacting with our prototype while we observed and interviewed them. We collected both quantitative performance data and qualitative feedback to identify friction points.

After the internal and panel reviews, I revisited the mid-fidelity stage individually using the feedback.

Bringing it to life (High Fidelity)

My guiding principle for the App's color system was "Content is King." This philosophy prioritizes the visibility, readability, and prominence of the content above all else.

Monochromatic Color Palette and Design

Midnight Green

allows vivid museum imagery to stand out, aligning with museums, culture, and serious outings over cheap coupons.

Azure accent

clearly signals loading states without secondary hues or confusing messaging.

Neutrals for the rest

ensure all interactive/content elements meet maximum readability and WCAG accessibility.

Designing Components based on User Needs

Users can now explore, save, and share museum deals from one unified feed, or plan their visit visually through a calendar-based planner. The design balanced information density with hierarchy, keeping Mary (and users like her) in mind throughout.

What I Learned

Users may articulate wants that seem simple, but their actual behaviors reveal subtle mental barriers.

“People don’t know what they want until you show it to them.”

-- Steve Jobs, in 1998

Working on this app brought this truth to life.
Mary, the Museum Go-er, guided the design from the start. She expressed her wants: “I want to know where the discounts are,” “I want my friends to come with me,” “I want it easier on my phone.”
Her actual needs, however, centered on clarity, certainty, and low-friction planning. Translating her wants into needs revealed that features like a unified feed, calendar-based planning, and effortless sharing weren’t simply us applying the MoSCoW framework; they directly addressed the mental barriers preventing her visits.
A clear learning moment came from the Discover section. Everyone wanted visual indicators that reduced search times. While the map tested positively, it conflicted with Mary’s preference for automation over exploration and highlighted implementation challenges, since most GLAM institutions aren’t affiliated.
Converting the Discover section into a search-first page simplified the experience, addressed her needs more effectively, and reinforced a critical insight: users’ stated wants may obscure the real barriers that influence behavior.
