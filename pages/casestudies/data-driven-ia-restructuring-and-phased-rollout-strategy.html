Isolating Variables: A Phased Rollout Strategy for IA Redesign

Delivered Nov 2023

Role: UX Strategist & Researcher (team of 4 UXRs)

Client: Textile Museum of Canada

Tools: OptimalSort, Treejack, Miro

Summary: The museum needed to improve their site's content discoverability. While the conventional solution was a UI overhaul, I proposed a phased implementation strategy to isolate improvements, ensuring the right attribution.

Every site needs to ensure their users go "Ahh, There it is!" and not a "God, Is this even on here!?"

The Textile Museum of Canada faced a disconnect between their rich content library and user engagement.

The initial request to our MI cohort was broad: "Fix the architecture." However, the underlying risk was that changing the structure with input from test users without understanding the real user's mental model could alienate the existing loyal user base. We could get real users to test the IA, but what if their users have a workaround in place for any problematic architecture? Doing so would result in existing patterns and behaviours clouding the test results, making the Museum feel like the Titanic.

Understanding the Mental Model

Before proposing solutions, we needed to validate the problem. We utilized open card sorting (n=8) and tree testing to map the existing architecture.

↦ Users consistently grouped "Privacy Policy" and "Team" under "About Us," validating usual conventions.
↦ Abstract labels like "TXTilecity" and "Cloth Clay" had high failure rates; their jargon was blocking discovery.
↦ Users expected donation pathways to be distinct from browsing, suggesting a need for a two-tiered navigation menu.

Audit & Data Gathering

Benchmarking

To prove that the issue was structural, we conducted Tree Testing on the existing site's IA.

We stripped away all visual design, testing labels and hierarchy only. This ensured that the failures recorded were purely semantic.

Our Diagnosis

Our data confirmed the hypothesis: The IA itself was broken. Even without the restrictive hamburger menu, users could not find key information.

The Strategic Recommendation

Based on the data, we developed a new Information Architecture. However, simply handing it was not enough for us. My teammates and I had a disussion where we felt like we needed to ensure this was practical and not theoretical.

The Risk of a Overhaul

The conventional wisdom that most of our Cohort mates also suggested (other teams working on the same issue) was to launch the New IA inside a new "Mega Menu" UI simultaneously. I advised the clients against this due to the risk of misunderstanding what actually works.

If metrics changed after a simultaneous launch, no one could attribute the change to the structure (IA) or the interface (UI).

What I proposed

I designed a phased A/B testing roadmap with interface mockups. This strategy allows them to validate our proposed IA before committing to development.

Phase A (Validation): Implement the New IA labels within the existing menu design to reduce UI bias.
Phase B (Optimization): Once the IA is validated, roll out the Mega Menu UI.

What I Learned

This project reinforced that how we implement is just as important as the change itself.

The Scientific Method

By testing the Information Architecture separate from the Visual Design, we created a way to attribute impact to the right change. If metrics improved in Phase A, we credit the IA. In Phase B, we credit the UI.
