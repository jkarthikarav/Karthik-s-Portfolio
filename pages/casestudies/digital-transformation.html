The Challenge

Fix It In Post Studios (FIIP Studios) wondered: "How do you introduce a technology that your own team fears will replace them?"

Breaking Through AI Anxiety in a Fast-Moving Industry

While competitors chased quick wins, FIIP Studios wanted a principled plan that would set them apart rather than leave them overexposed when the hype faded.

In 2022–23, the industry was hit by Generative AI disruption: OpenAI, Midjourney, and Adobe burst onto the scene, and every client wanted you to use them.

Designers inside the studio were asking whether their roles would survive the transition.

A rushed adoption risked burning credibility with both clients and staff.

The Framework

A Framework-First Strategy for Responsible AI Adoption

Through structured research and workflow analysis, I developed four core design guidelines and validated them through a live pilot project. Each principle reflects a deliberate choice about what to prioritize. They also relied on eliminating approaches that might give AI too much authority or too little use.

Copilot-style infrastructure
AI to be embedded into existing workflows to accelerate output speed, operating as infrastructure.

Quantity, not Quality
Prioritize rapid generation of options during early-stages, where stakeholders require volume, not finished assets.

Focus on specific phases
AI can be scoped to specific tasks rather than applied across the entire pipeline.

Changes without restarting
Workflows will be redesigned to eliminate inherent issues with AI generation (having to rebuild entire outputs).

Opportunity: Client Work

In May 2023, a movie director approached FIIP Studios to create an animated Pre-viz video to help align producers and crew before committing to production.

One strong idea emerged from applying the design guidelines.

Use AI inside structured workflows to mass produce swappable visual assets during early-stage exploration. Our Mass production idea ensures that designers still had to evaluate AI outputs to ensure quality and usability. This did however result in difficult conversations early on about the limits of generative output and the Creative Director had to actively manage client expectations around that.

Full generation was possible. But if AI produced the final asset, no one could really own it. This would also have delivered faster results on paper but speed without oversight isn't effective, both come at a cost to the other.

Even 2 years after this decision, studies by other researchers and companies show that hybrid workflows are better than full automation for creative tasks.

Understanding the As-Is System

I was asked to create a concrete, billable service validated against a live client project - a PreViz to market a Movie to Investors.

Firstly, I studied the Current "As-Is" Workflow.

The traditional pre-visualization workflow had a structural flaw: the Director was the center of every decision, making the studio dependent on their ability to articulate.

When a Director couldn't articulate their vision, the studio sat idle or worse, made progress down the wrong aisle.

Without understanding the studio's technical capabilities, creative changes might need a near-complete restart.

And vague briefs compounded over time, becoming excessive last-minute revisions that devastate timelines and trust.

Fig 1. "As-Is" workflow: Every decision gate required Director input; so every delay was a Director delay.

Fixing the workflow wasn't simply adding an AI tool, it required Directors to give something up.

Redesigning the Service

For the live pilot project, we used Adobe Photoshop's Generative Fill feature, which was BETA-launched two weeks prior.

I proposed a blueprint that works when creative direction is established early and held.

I intentionally shifted the technical burden to the Studio (Guideline 4), made decision-making faster by allowing alternation (Guideline 2), and enabled studio to vet AI outputs before polish began (Guideline 1).

The Studio gained control over pacing, but that control came with accountability it hadn't previously held.

Fig 2. "To-Be" workflow: Reducing perpetual client involvement in the production phase was my goal.

The pilot confirmed that the redesigned workflow held under real production pressure (Guideline 3).

Taking on the technical burden meant the Studio had to be confident enough to make early creative decisions without Director input.

The Director retained creative authority over tone and final approval, but saw less of the work-in-progress, which some find uncomfortable.

How did the Blueprint unfold in real time?

Tone Alignment

Rather than waiting for a brief, we opened with a facilitated alignment session.

It also requires trust the studio has to earn. This session was the highest-risk moment in the workflow.

Scene Breakdown

The boundary between AI-appropriate and human-led assets isn't always obvious.

The PM and Design Lead worked out (per shot) what AI could reliably produce.

Designing with AI

Generative Fill was in beta. So, evaluation criteria had to come from the Design lead.

Junior designers mass-produced environments while our sketch artists sketched characters.

QA & Compositing to Edit Floor

Final assets were reviewed against the established creative direction, then exported as layered passes to give the edit team flexibility for animation and VFX.

What I Learned

A structured, principles-driven approach to AI adoption can transform creative operations without sacrificing human-centred design principles or output quality under commercial pressure.
The studio entered this project with a reactive/dependent workflow, and left with a structured service it could repeat.
A fully automated pipeline would have been faster. But, keeping humans in the evaluation loop protected creative ownership and that is what made the output trustworthy enough for the client.
The live project delivered two weeks ahead of schedule, despite the team building the process in real time.
Oversimplified AI approaches often erase critical labour and torpedo traditional avenues for mentorship and workforce development. In this project, junior designers were tasked with generating using AI. This was not to cut costs, but so that they would develop design thinking skills rather than learning tools that AI would eventually replace.
The result was billable, repeatable, and adopted as the studio's standard operating model, including structured prompt libraries, QA checkpoints, and clearly defined task boundaries.
