Driving Student Recruitment for one of the top Engineering departments in Canada and the world

ECE at U of T's students were not navigating to core informational pages and stakeholder trust in the website had eroded. I redesigned and rebuilt multiple core pages to address a missed recruitment opportunity by shifting to a student-focused hierarchy, giving them a functional and aesthetic facelift.

My role was Designing & Developing Front-End on WordPress

From June 2024 to March 2025, I handled accessibility fixes and web production requests, tightening user journeys to align with both WCAG standards and U of T's Brand guidelines, across both the public website and intranet.

The Challenge

—

For the first 7 months, standardising UI was a priority and I had focused on ensuring non-technical staff could maintain what was built without breaking it. In November 2024, I was tasked with rebuilding core pages on the ECE website, but where would I even start.

The Department did have a pain point to start with. ECE's website served everyone. That meant it worked for no one in particular... least of all, the Students.

Pictured Above: What the Home page looked like in 2024.

93% of high-school students use a university website during their decision-making journey. [Source] But, ECE's wasn't built for them. The homepage had no clear primary audience. Prospective students with the highest stakes had no clear path through any of it.

Three pain points shaped the scope: outdated content, broken user journeys, and visual misalignment with the broader U of T brand.

What did I do first?

My process involved Content Audits and Discovery, wherein I understand common industry patterns/mental models and identify content gaps.
I didn't design the homepage in isolation, doing so would have sent prospective students to dead ends faster. An audit of ECE and comparable engineering department websites revealed multiple insights.
Visually, ECE's site was inconsistent with U of T at large. Content overlapped, but entire content categories were absent. For eg, UBC's Engineering asked for capstone proposals. Waterloo's was patent-focused. U of T's ECE did not have one.
We decided to prioritize first-time users and treat prospective students as the primary audience, with elements/content for other audiences needing to also serve them. Any content that couldn't was deprioritised.

What I Learned

A great homepage pointing to underdeveloped internal pages would have replaced a solvable problem with a structural one.
Sites that look right at launch but are too fragile for non-technical staff to update will eventually break down.
Academic Institutions often design primarily for maintainability, resulting in sites not optimised for the people who need them.
Designing with prospective students as the primary audience solved both problems at once. Every request could be evaluated against whether it served prospective students. When the answer was no, designers can push back rather than quoting subjective preferences.
