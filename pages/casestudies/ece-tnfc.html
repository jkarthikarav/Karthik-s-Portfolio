Migrating a Website, Simplifying Access

For: the University of Toronto, The Edward S. Rogers Sr. Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering (ECE at U of T)

Shipped Mar, 2025

Summary: Case study documenting the process to migrate the previously independent TNFC lab's website into the main ECE site, unifying digital presence.

The ECE department had a slowly bubbling up concern: the TNFC lab.

Although the TNFC lab is known to be a department-operated lab, its digital presence still conveyed a lack of connection. The core problem was not visual. The TNFC website suffered from content fragmentation, unclear language, and buried compliance requirements that made access difficult.

My primary responsibility was restructuring and rewriting existing content. UI design was used to support these content decisions.

This was my final project at ECE.

My Integration Strategy

Before opening Figma, I conducted a content audit of the existing TNFC website.

The Original Website's Homepage

Each page was reviewed for purpose, audience, and required user action, with a focus on simplifying wherever procedural and compliance information was written, sequenced, and surfaced.

Much of the content assumed prior knowledge or existed only to fill inherited WordPress templates.

The site contained roughly fifteen pages across five top-level navigation items, but critical information was fragmented across multiple subpages. Users were required to click repeatedly to understand how to access the lab, what training was mandatory, and which forms were required. This structure obscured the lab’s value and increased the risk of users missing required steps.

The Original Website's User Access Pages

The Original Website's User Access Sub Page

All the first level navigation items were in the same template as the User Access page, and the sub pages in the same template. I was led to understand that these were a generic blog style template available on WordPress.

Content consolidation was driven by simplifying user intent. Descriptive content answering what TNFC is, what it offers, and who it serves was grouped into a single overview page. Procedural content answering how to gain access, including forms, fees, training, and billing, was rewritten and consolidated into a dedicated User access page.

Key Findings from the Audit

• Content was written as descriptive narrative & unnecessarily divided across multiple pages.

• Navigation was deep, burying important actions within paragraphs rather than surfacing them.

• Pages mixed audiences without signaling which content applied to which users.

• Visual design, Language, and Site Layouts reflected internal organizational decisions, not ECE or UofT guidelines.

What the Department and Lab Were Looking For

Clear Lab Identity

Position TNFC clearly within ECE, showing it as an integral research facility rather than a standalone lab.

Easy Access

How could users quickly find usage policies, educational resources, and regulations?

UI Design

The original TNFC website relied heavily on long-form paragraphs and unecessarily deep navigation to display their user access requirements. Mandatory steps such as agreements, forms, and training videos were embedded within hyperlinked text, making it easy for users to miss them. These are the three most crucial things I aimed to address directly through both the rewritten content and the UI.

Key UI Decisions - TNFC (Main Page)

• The hero immediately communicates TNFC’s scale, cleanroom environment, and technical sophistication.

• Structured sections (intro, research areas, facility highlights) allow users to quickly understand the lab’s scope and capabilities.

• Additional CTAs (“User Access,” “Client Services,” “Contact TNFC”) use action-oriented language.

• Including contact details for faculty and technical staff supports engagement.

The Initially Approved mockup.

Key UI Decisions - TNFC (Access Page)

• Clear listing of mandatory forms and training ensures users understand compliance requirements upfront.

• Financial and billing information is presented concisely, with discounts clearly communicated for qualifying researchers.

• Quick links to PDF forms (instead of hyperlinks in the original) make essential resources easy to find.

Web Development

I built the TNFC pages directly in WordPress while adhering to the University’s brand and accessibility guidelines. Components were designed to be modular, allowing marketing or web production teams (at the time, also me) to update images, PDFs, or text without breaking layouts or requiring developer intervention.

Why This Page Mattered

This was not just a webpage for a research lab. This project was about designing language and structure that made the website more usable.
TNFC had long existed as a separate digital entity. Its website reflected this with its own independent news and blog feed.
I was assigned this, my last project for the department, in February 2025. The pages went live by late March/early April. My work relied on content auditing and stakeholder workshops. I identified what mattered, consolidated what did not, and reduced more than fifteen pages into two core pages that communicated more with less.
Every design decision was driven by compliance and regulation, including access, training, billing, and safety. At the same time, the pages were designed to feel native to ECE while clearly articulating TNFC’s value. The result positions TNFC as a core, high-value research facility within the department.
A Humble Brag: The pages were built with modular, reusable components to support future migrations across ECE research labs, if any. Long after my departure, they launched the Grid Modernization Centre page, which reused multiple components and the custom hero banner originally designed for TNFC, demonstrating the strength of my designs and my forward-looking systems design.
