I researched, designed, prototyped, rebuilt, and shipped ECE at U of T's Nanofabrication research lab website, converting it from a standalone website with inconsistent branding, to a responsive content hub integrated into ECE's public-facing website, working across phone, tablet, and desktop.

Context

From an isolated lab website to part of ECE's digital ecosystem.

TNFC had its own website and no connection to ECE's brand, navigation, or broader digital ecosystem.

The challenge was not to visually overhaul the lab. It was to make TNFC work as part of the system ECE already had, while making it easier for people to discover and access the facility.

“The problem wasn’t that the lab had a standalone website. The problem was that the website made the lab harder to find, understand, and use.”

Building an Integrated Experience

Defining the information architecture around user needs

The existing site featured numerous top-level pages that functioned primarily as link aggregators, burying key information within sub-pages.

By assessing the existing content against the needs of TNFC users, I identified two primary user flows: understanding what TNFC is and what it offers, versus finding the information needed to use the facility.

TNFC Content Hub Home → TNFC User Access →

Designing information hierarchy for discovery

Crucial content, such as the two lab facilities, was previously buried under "About Us."

I identified this as a discoverability issue and recommended surfacing the facilities directly within the homepage experience. This became a structured card grid that allowed users to recognize available facilities without navigating through multiple levels.

Streamlining the user access journey

User Access information was previously spread across five distinct sub-pages covering forms, training, fees, policies, and compliance.

Mapping these requirements revealed a fragmented path for users trying to access the facility. I consolidated the information into a single User Access page to create a more coherent onboarding experience.

Where lengthy content—such as Access Fees tables—did not suit the interface, I recommended they be provided as a downloadable PDF linked directly from the page.

Designing visual hierarchy to prioritize important content

Partner organizations were previously listed as simple text links on a separate Partners page. I used visual hierarchy to give these relationships greater prominence within the overall experience, moving them to the homepage as a three-column section featuring an auto-scrolling logo gallery.

Essentially, I decided what deserved prominence and designed the interface accordingly.

The same principle informed how content was presented throughout: important information needed to be visually discoverable, not simply available somewhere in a sub-page on the site.

Designing reusable components for a scalable system

Because ECE lacked a shared component library at the time, interface patterns were frequently rebuilt.

I designed the TNFC components as modular, repeatable patterns that could be extended rather than recreated. For eg, The grids containing the cards for Facilities and Services could be extended rather than creating a new card each time—allowing work to extend beyond TNFC and into the wider ECE at U of T ecosystem.

Following this, components and patterns from the TNFC pages—including most sections of the home page—were successfully reused for integrating another ECE web property, the Grid Modernization Centre.

Designing a responsive experience across devices

No one interacts from a single device. I designed the content hub responsively from the beginning, adapting layouts and components across mobile, tablet, laptop, and desktop screen sizes.

Reflections

Every design decision was driven by the need to ensure ease of access to the regulations, including access, training, billing, and safety. At the same time, the pages were designed to feel native to ECE while also expressing TNFC’s identity and values. The result positions TNFC as a core, high-value research facility within the department.

The prototyping for the TNFC homepage and its intial sub-page was done in under two weeks—because I wanted working drafts in front of stakeholders early.

Why? There were a couple of design decisions that non-design stakeholders couldn't envision at first. My working drafts and prototype were instrumental in ensuring they weren't approving something they misunderstood or required major rework late in the process.

The approvals process took much longer than anticipated which caused the project to be in the final phases of web development during the final week of my contract—causing a rushed production of assets and finalization/visual polish. Other delays were mostly scheduling related and not design/development related.

After I left, development on other parts of the site reused components and patterns that were originally designed for TNFC's section, including the Grid Modernization Centre's new page. The patterns were picked up and used after I left—not only were my components and patterns liked—the structural decisions made sense and they were also repeatable.
