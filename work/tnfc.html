Making a Research Facility Easier to Discover and Access

I researched, designed, and developed a responsive content hub for the Toronto NanoFabrication Centre (TNFC) at the University of Toronto's Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering (ECE). The project brought the lab's previously standalone website into ECE's existing digital ecosystem, improving how its facilities, services, and access requirements were organized and presented.

Information Architecture

Defining the information architecture around user needs

The existing site had numerous top-level pages that primarily acted as link aggregators, leaving important information buried within sub-pages.

By assessing the existing content against user needs, I identified two primary flows:

  • Discover TNFC: Understand what the facility is and what it offers.
  • Access TNFC: Find the information required to use the facility.

These flows informed how content was organized, prioritized, and connected throughout the new content hub.

Designing information hierarchy for discovery

The two lab facilities were presented under "About Us", making them harder to discover within the existing site structure.

I recommended surfacing them directly on the homepage. A structured card grid gave each facility a clear place in the information hierarchy, allowing visitors to recognize the available facilities without navigating through multiple levels.

Streamlining the user access journey

Information required to access TNFC's facilities was distributed across five sub-pages covering forms, training, fees, policies, and compliance.

I consolidated these requirements into a single User Access page, bringing the information needed to understand the process into one place.

For lengthy content that was less suited to the page layout, such as access-fee tables, I recommended the content team create downloadable PDFs linked directly from the page.

Designing visual hierarchy to prioritize important content

Partner organizations were previously listed as text links on a separate Partners page, giving these relationships limited prominence in the overall experience.

I moved the partners section to the homepage and designed a three-column layout featuring an auto-scrolling logo gallery. This gave the organizations a more visible place within the content hub and made their relationships with TNFC easier to recognize.

The same principle guided the broader interface: prioritizing information based on its importance rather than leaving it buried within secondary pages.

Designing reusable components for a scalable system

ECE did not have a shared component library at the time, so interface patterns were often rebuilt across web properties.

I designed TNFC's components as modular, repeatable patterns that could be extended rather than recreated. For eg, the facility and service card grids could accommodate additional content without requiring an entirely new interface pattern.

These components and patterns were subsequently reused when integrating another ECE web property, the Grid Modernization Centre. This extended the value of the work beyond TNFC and demonstrated how its interface patterns could support other facilities within ECE's digital ecosystem.

Designing a responsive experience across devices

I designed and implemented the content hub to adapt across mobile, tablet, laptop, and desktop screen sizes. Layouts and components were adjusted to maintain a coherent experience across viewports rather than treating the desktop design as the only reference.

Reflections

Every design decision was driven by the need to ensure ease of access to the regulations, including access, training, billing, and safety. At the same time, the pages were designed to feel native to ECE while also expressing TNFC’s identity and values. The result positions TNFC as a core, high-value research facility within the department.

The prototyping for the TNFC homepage and its initial sub-page was done in under two weeks—because I wanted working drafts in front of stakeholders early.

Why? There were a couple of design decisions that non-design stakeholders couldn't envision at first. My working drafts and prototype were instrumental in ensuring they weren't approving something they misunderstood or required major rework late in the process.

The approvals process took much longer than anticipated, which caused the project to be in the final phases of web development during the final week of my contract—causing a rushed production of assets and finalization/visual polish. Other delays were mostly scheduling related and not design/development related.

After I left, development on other parts of the site reused components and patterns that were originally designed for TNFC's section, including the Grid Modernization Centre's new page. The patterns were picked up and used after I left—not only were my components and patterns liked—the structural decisions made sense and they were also repeatable.
