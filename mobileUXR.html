Mobile App UX Research for Ticket Deals

Museum-goers in Toronto often miss out on free or discounted tickets because deal info is scattered, hard to find, or outdated. Most give up mid-search.

Project Context

Domain & Focus

We (a team of 6 UXRs) studied accessibility challenges for Torontonians in the arts and culture domain, focusing specifically on how people discover and use museum ticket deals.

Problem Identification

Desk research revealed that eligible visitors often miss out on free or discounted admission because deal information is:
1. Scattered across multiple sites
2. Hard to verify
3. Inconsistent and outdated

Goal

Reduce fragmentation and create a mobile-first platform where deals are easy to discover and act upon.

Solution Approach

To design the solution, we conducted UX research to understand users’ behaviors, pain points, and needs when accessing museum ticket deals.

UX Research Findings

Research Goals

1. Understand user awareness and behavior regarding museum ticket discounts.
2. Identify friction points and reasons for drop-off.
3. Explore trust indicators in digital cultural experiences.
4. Highlight accessibility and inclusivity gaps.

Research Process

We conducted qualitative interviews and contextual inquiries to understand user behavior and challenges. Our target audiences were:

Students

Newcomers

Families

Seniors

We also examined museum websites & ticketing flows (competitive audit) and analysed deal discovery behaviours.

Our Insights
Low Awareness of Deals

Discounts exist but are poorly publicised; many audiences simply don’t know.

Perceived Effort > Benefit

Participants abandon searches if offers aren’t easy to spot or verify.

Inconsistent Digital Flows

Different museums have entirely different booking and discount layouts.

Fragmented Information

No single source aggregates deal data or event calendars.

UXR to UI Design Process

Meet Mary,
the Museum Go-er!
"I would visit more museums if
I had free or discounted tickets"

Mary was a fictitious human birthed by the group's research data. A hard-working and busy student, when time allows, she opts to visit museums with her friends or even solo!

Mary’s attempt to visit a museum in the modern day and age mirrors the challenges faced by many Torontonians. Despite prolonged efforts to find discounted tickets, the benefits are overshadowed by the time spent searching.

The delay results in exceeding her budget, highlighting the difficulties in navigating the process of obtaining affordable tickets for museum visits.

Ideation & Feature Prioritization

Mary’s needs shaped our Impact vs. Feasibility matrix for feature prioritization.

The Designs

I (alongside one other member of the UXR team) designed a mobile UI to make this complexity invisible through:

1. Ease of Access & Speed

Insight: Users want quick, centralized access to all ticket discounts.
Design: Centralized feed with all available discounts in one screen and Save & Share CTAs for instant action.

2. Planning by Date

Insight: Users plan visits by specific dates.
Design: Calendar-based visual planner for easy date selection.

3. Trust & Clarity

Insight: Users trust interfaces that are clear and organized.
Design: Offers grouped visually with recognizable icons for quick comprehension.

4. Relevant Recommendations

Insight: Users need personalized, relevant content.
Design: AI-powered recommendations based on previous behavior.

UI Design Process - Low Fidelity

Designs were iterated based on internal review and presented to a panel of senior UX researchers during a "Design Review" session at the iSchool, University of Toronto.

I later revisited these designs based on the feedback received.

UI Design Process - High Fidelity

What I Learned

The goal of this project was to create a clean, functional UI that simplifies how Torontonians discover and access museum ticket deals.

Designing around real-world fragmentation (many museums, different systems) required a UI that could bring coherence without overwhelming the user. Through visual hierarchy, smart filtering, and minimal navigation, we created a prototype that felt both local and user-friendly. This project also sharpened my feature prioritization skills using an impact-feasibility matrix, and reinforced the value of designing for trust.

Also, I envisioned it as an AI-powered interface: its recommendations and event highlights would adapt based on user preferences and behaviour data, improving relevance over time.

Key Takeaway: Make messy systems feel simple

When back-end data is scattered or inconsistent, design clarity is non-negotiable. A well-structured interface can build trust by masking complexity, guiding users straight to what they value.
